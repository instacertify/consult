Worth knowing before you pay for anything

Nobody issues an "IP certificate". Here's what you're actually buying.

An IP rating is not granted by a government department and there is no central register of it. It is a test result. A laboratory runs the tests written in IEC 60529 on your sample, issues a report saying it passed at a stated rating, and you then declare that rating on the product and in your literature.

So the only things that carry any weight are the lab that did the work, the scope it is accredited for, and whether the test conditions in the report match what you are claiming. That is the whole job — and it is where it usually goes wrong, because a report from a lab without the right scope is a report your buyer, your BIS filing or your tender officer can simply refuse.

How it usually goes
  • A rating is picked off a competitor's datasheet rather than from where the product actually sits
  • Samples go to whichever lab answered the phone, scope unchecked
  • It fails on a gasket or a cable gland nobody looked at first
  • New samples, new slot, second invoice — and the launch date moves
How we do it
  • Set the rating from the real duty — where it is mounted, what hits it, who is asking.
  • Check the lab's scope against your rating, not just its name — including size of chamber and the IPX9 rig if you need it.
  • Review the sample before it ships — seals, glands, vents, drain paths, breather placement.
  • Read the report before you publish it — so the rating on your box matches the conditions on page two.

Four things people get wrong

Most failed IP tests are failures of the paperwork, not the product

These four come up on almost every first call.

1

IP67 does not include IP65

Immersion and jetting are different physics. A sealed box can sit in a metre of still water for half an hour and still let a pressure jet past a joint. Up to IPX6 the levels build on each other — IPX7 and IPX8 do not. If your product needs both, it is declared as a pair, such as IP66/IP68, and both tests are booked and paid for.

2

X does not mean zero

IPX5 means water was tested and solids were not declared. IP6X means the opposite. Writing IPX5 when you meant IP65 quietly drops your dust claim, and buyers who read specs properly will notice. If both digits matter to you, both have to be tested.

3

"Waterproof" is not an IP term

IEC 60529 describes what happened under a defined test for a defined time. It is not a promise about hot water, chemicals, steam, pressure changes or years of UV. Advertising a product as waterproof when the report says IPX7 is a claim you cannot support if it is ever questioned.

4

IP 69K is a different standard

The 80 °C, 80-bar close-range washdown test is written into ISO 20653 for road vehicles and appears in IEC 60529 as IPX9. They are close but not identical, and a buyer asking for one will not always accept a report written against the other. We confirm which one your customer means before booking.

Where the demand comes from

Who is asking you for an IP rating

The reason behind the request usually decides which lab the report has to come from — so it is the first thing we ask.

A BIS filingISI mark or CRS registration

Several product standards carry an IP requirement inside them — luminaires, control gear, enclosures, charging equipment. Where the filing is BIS, the test report generally has to come from a BIS-recognised laboratory, not merely an accredited one. A perfectly valid NABL report from the wrong lab gets the application returned.

A tender or a government buyerPublic procurement

Tender specs name a rating and often name the accreditation too. We read the clause before quoting, because the difference between "IP65" and "IP65 tested at an NABL laboratory with the report in the bidder's own name" is the difference between qualifying and being disqualified on technicals.

An export customerEU, Middle East, US buyers

Overseas buyers usually want a report they can read in English against IEC 60529 or EN 60529, with the sample clearly identified and photographs of it on the rig. We make sure the report is written so it survives being forwarded to someone who has never heard of the lab.

A marketplace listingAmazon, Flipkart and retail chains

Platforms increasingly ask for evidence behind a durability claim on the listing. A test report at the exact rating printed on the packaging is what closes that, and it needs to name the model that is actually on sale.

Your own engineeringBefore you commit to tooling

The cheapest time to find out a gasket groove is wrong is before the mould is cut. A short pre-compliance run on a prototype costs a fraction of a failed formal test plus a tooling change, and nothing about it has to be declared.

Product types

What we most often put on the rig

Lighting & luminaires

Street lights, flood lights, high bays, façade and landscape fittings, downlights and drivers. Typically IP65 or IP66, and often tested alongside the luminaire standard rather than on its own.

Enclosures & panels

Control panels, junction boxes, feeder pillars, meter boxes, distribution boards. The failures here are nearly always the gland plate, the door seal or a vent that was added late.

EV charging & batteries

AC and DC charge points, connectors, battery packs and BMS housings. Usually IP65 upward, and frequently required alongside the electrical safety testing rather than after it.

Consumer electronics

Handsets, earbuds, wearables, smart home devices, trackers. IP67 and IP68 dominate, and the declared IPX8 depth and duration need deciding before the sample is sent.

Industrial & food plant

Motors, sensors, weighing equipment, conveyors and anything that meets a hose or a steam cleaner. This is where IPX6, IPX9 and IP 69K actually earn their keep.

Solar & outdoor systems

Junction boxes, inverters, combiner boxes, string monitoring, outdoor CCTV and telecom cabinets. Long UV and weather exposure means the seal choice matters as much as the rating.

Why it matters

A rating you cannot evidence is worse than no rating at all

Printing IP66 on a product is a claim. If the report behind it was run at a lower rating, or on a different variant, or at a lab without that scope, the claim is standing on nothing.

That surfaces at the worst moments — a tender technical evaluation, a customer audit, a warranty dispute over water damage, or a platform asking for evidence behind a listing.

FAQ

Questions we get on the first call

Is IP testing mandatory in India?

Not on its own. There is no standalone law that says every product must carry an IP rating.

What happens instead is that IP requirements sit inside other things — a product standard you are certifying to, a tender specification, a buyer's purchase condition, or a marketplace's evidence policy. So whether it is compulsory for you depends entirely on which of those applies. That is the first thing we establish, because it also decides which lab the report has to come from.

What rating does my product need?

Work backwards from where it lives and what reaches it.

Indoors, dust only, no water — IP5X or IP54 is usually honest. Outdoors in rain, mounted on a pole or wall — IP65 or IP66. Handheld or portable, where being dropped in water is a real risk — IP67. Permanently submerged or buried — IP68, with the depth and duration you declare. Hosed down with hot water — IPX9 or IP 69K.

The commonest error is going higher than the duty needs. Every step up costs money in sealing, in tooling and in test, and buyers rarely pay more for a rating they did not ask for.

How long does IP testing take?

The tests themselves are short. Solids probes take under an hour. The dust chamber runs for 8 hours. Most water tests are between 3 and 30 minutes of actual exposure.

What sets the calendar is lab queue and sample logistics, not the test. Plan for one to two weeks in normal conditions once samples reach the lab, longer if you need a rig that is in demand or a chamber big enough for a large enclosure. We tell you the realistic date when we quote, not the optimistic one.

Does an IP rating expire?

The test report does not expire by itself. What ends its usefulness is a change to the product.

If you change the enclosure, the gasket material, a gland, a vent, a fastener pattern or the moulding tool, the report no longer describes what you are selling and the rating should be retested. Some certification schemes that reference the report also set their own validity periods, which is a separate matter from the report itself.

Can one product carry two ratings?

Yes, and for many products it is the correct way to declare it.

Because IPX7 and IPX8 do not include the jet tests, a product that has to survive both a hose and immersion is declared as a pair — IP66/IP68, for example — with both tests run and both shown on the report. Declaring only IP68 on such a product quietly leaves the hose-down claim unevidenced.

What does IP testing cost?

There are two numbers and we always show them separately.

The lab charge, which depends on the rating, the size and weight of your sample and which rigs it needs — a dust chamber run plus IPX9 costs very differently from a single IPX4. And our fee, for the rating assessment, the lab matching, the sample review and managing the file through to the report.

You see the lab's figure before we book, not after. If a retest becomes necessary we requote it honestly rather than treating it as included.

Can you test a prototype before we commit to tooling?

Yes, and for anything with a moulded enclosure we usually suggest it.

A pre-compliance run on a prototype is faster, cheaper and does not have to be declared anywhere. If it fails, you learn where the water gets in while the tool can still be changed. The formal test then follows on production samples.

Do you do the testing yourselves?

No, and we would be careful of anyone who says they do both.

Testing is done by accredited laboratories.
